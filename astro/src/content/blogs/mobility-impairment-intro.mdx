##  What Is Mobility Impairment?

Mobility impairment is a broad term. It can make walking, standing, moving, or using your arms and hands harder. It may affect one part of the body or several. A person may be born with a mobility impairment or develop one later in life.

Mobility impairment can include:

* Trouble walking, standing, or keeping balance
*  Limited use of the arms, hands, or fingers
* Pain or stiffness that limits movement
* Full or partial paralysis

There are many causes. Some people are born with conditions such as cerebral palsy or spina bifida. Others develop a mobility impairment after an injury, such as a spinal cord injury, or because of a condition such as arthritis, multiple sclerosis, or muscular dystrophy. Some people have lost an arm or a leg.

Mobility impairment does not look the same for everyone. Some impairments stay the same over time. Others can change from day to day. Two people with the same diagnosis may have very different experiences.

## Mobility Impairment Can Look Different From Day to Day

There is no single mobility impairment experience. What a person can do may depend on the space around them, their energy, pain, and the tools they use.

### Getting Around

*  Some people use canes, walkers, crutches, or wheelchairs. They may use them all the time, sometimes, or only when symptoms get worse.
*  Ramps, elevators, and curb cuts can make it possible to get from one place to another.
*  Stairs, long distances, and uneven ground can create barriers.

### Daily Tasks

*  Limited grip or hand movement can make writing, typing, or opening a jar harder.
*  Adaptive tools, built-up handles, and voice-controlled devices can make tasks easier.
*  The amount of help a person needs can change from day to day.

### Fatigue and Pain

*  Moving through spaces that were not designed for you can take extra energy.
*  Fatigue is common for many people with mobility impairments.
*  Pain can come and go without warning.
*  Rest breaks and flexible schedules can make a big difference.

### Social and Emotional Experience

*  People may be left out when spaces are not accessible.
*  Assumptions about what someone can or cannot do can be hurtful and wrong.
*  People often have more confidence when spaces and systems are designed with access in mind.

These barriers are not a personal failure. Often, the problem is due to the environment, not the person. When spaces and systems are designed for access, more people can take part.

## Mobility Impairment at Every Stage of Life

Mobility impairment can affect people at any age. People with mobility impairments are children, working adults, and older adults. They work in every field and live in every kind of community.

Needs can change over time. A child may need support at school. A working adult may need workplace accommodations. An older adult may develop a mobility impairment later in life.

Access to health care, helpful technology, and inclusive spaces matters at every age. A mobility impairment does not decide what a person can do. Access can make the difference.

## Language Matters

People use different words to describe themselves. Some prefer “person with a mobility impairment” or “person with a physical disability.” Others prefer “disabled person” or “physically disabled person.” Use the words a person chooses for themselves.

Avoid language that presents mobility impairment as a tragedy. For example, avoid “wheelchair-bound,” “confined to a wheelchair,” and “suffering from.” A wheelchair is a mobility tool. Focus on the person, their choices, and the access they need.

## What Does Inclusion Look Like?

Inclusion is more than good intentions. It means removing barriers and making access part of everyday design.

*  Provide ramps, wide doorways, and accessible restrooms.
*  Keep aisles, walkways, and workspaces clear.
*  Offer flexible schedules and remote-work options when possible.
*  Provide accessible parking near entrances.
*  Make sure furniture and counters work for people who use wheelchairs or other mobility aids.
*  Ask before helping. Never touch someone's mobility aid without permission.
*  Listen when people explain what they need. Pain and fatigue are not always visible.

These changes help people with mobility impairments take part fully. They can also make spaces easier to use for everyone.
