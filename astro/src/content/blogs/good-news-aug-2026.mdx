Every month, we gather stories from around the world that show what progress in inclusion and accessibility looks like in action, breakthroughs, bold initiatives, and communities leading the way. August 2026 brought four clear improvements for disabled people, from four different parts of the world.

A government removed a rule that had stopped disabled people travelling free in the morning. A concert venue built hearing access into the building itself. A regulator told an airline that its wheelchair policy was a barrier. And two founders in Nairobi built a navigation app for the streets they walk every day.

Here is what happened, and why each one matters.

##  1. England is removing the time limit on disabled bus passes

If you hold a disabled bus pass in England today, it works between 9:30am and 11pm. Outside those hours, you pay.

That 9:30am start sounds like a small detail. It is not. It means you cannot use the pass for an early shift, a first lecture, an 8am hospital appointment or a school run.

For anyone who relies on the bus, the pass stopped working at the hours that matter most for getting to work.

From 1 April 2027, the time limits go. The pass will work around the clock. The government has put £60 million into the change, split between the Department for Work and Pensions and the Department for Transport.

The change applies to the seven groups already eligible for a pass. They include:

- people who are blind or partially sighted
- people who are profoundly deaf
- people with a learning disability
- people who cannot walk because of a disability

This is a good example of something worth naming. The barrier here was never the bus. It was a time window written into a rule, and rewriting the rule removed it.

Read the [government announcement on free 24/7 bus travel for disabled people.](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/free-bus-travel-247-for-disabled-people-as-prime-minister-takes-further-action-to-ease-cost-of-living)

##  2. A South Korean arena is sending concert sound straight to hearing aids

Live music is hard to follow with hearing loss. Venues echo, crowds are loud, and hearing aids pick up all of it at once. The music and the noise arrive together.

Inspire Arena in Incheon has taken a different approach. It now sends the concert audio directly to hearing aids and cochlear implants using Auracast, a wireless standard built for exactly this. The sound travels from the sound desk to the listener, so the echo in the room does not affect it.

There is also a dedicated listening space at the venue, with staff available to help.

Inspire Entertainment Resort built the project with JYP Entertainment and the accessibility company Hearcycle. They called it Sound Beyond Hearing. The first shows featured artists including 2PM and Young K from DAY6.

The important part is not the technology itself. It is that the technology is built into the venue, so it is there for every show. Fans do not have to ask for it at each event and hope someone says yes.

Read the [Korea Times report on accessible concerts at Inspire Arena.](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/entertainment/20260824/jyp-inspire-team-up-to-make-concerts-more-accessible-for-people-with-hearing-loss)

##  3. Canada's regulator said an airline's wheelchair limit was a barrier

WestJet had a rule that it would not carry mobility aids weighing more than 300 pounds, which is about 136 kilograms. Many power wheelchairs weigh more than that. The rule meant something simple for the people who use those chairs: they could not fly.

In August the Canadian Transportation Agency ruled that the limit is an undue barrier. It ordered the airline to remove the limit before July 2027.

The regulator did not just say no. It also named practical fixes, including spreader plates that spread the weight across the cargo hold floor. It said the airline must arrange other travel options in the meantime.

The complaint came from Steven Fletcher, who lives in Winnipeg and is a former federal transport minister. He had been stopped from flying between Winnipeg and Toronto with his own chair.

This helps more than one person. A national regulator has now decided, in public and in writing, that an arbitrary weight number is a barrier rather than a safety fact. Other travellers can refer to that decision.

Read the [Canadian Press report on the WestJet wheelchair ruling.](https://globalnews.ca/news/12028302/westjet-ordered-to-scrap-wheelchair-weight-cap/)

##  4. A Nairobi app describes the street ahead

Sightra is a navigation app built in Nairobi by two Kenyan founders, Ruth Nzuki and Brian Gillo. It uses artificial intelligence to identify what is in front of a user. It then describes it out loud through an earpiece, along with route guidance.

The interesting choice is what it was trained on. Most object recognition tools are trained on streets in North America and Europe, so they expect even curbs, marked crossings and continuous pavements. Sightra was trained on Kenyan roads. It recognises open drains, missing curbs and informal crossings, which are the hazards its users actually meet.

It runs on an ordinary smartphone, with no special hardware to buy. It has been tested with schools for blind students and with individual users, including the lawyer Julius Mbura.

There is a lesson here for anyone building assistive technology. Tools work better when they are designed where they will be used, by people who know the local conditions.

Read the [Africanews story on the Sightra navigation app in Kenya.](https://www.africanews.com/2026/08/20/phone-app-helps-kenyas-visually-impaired-get-around/)

## What these four have in common

These four stories look unrelated: a benefit rule, a sound system, a legal ruling and an app.

But each one started the same way. Somebody named a specific barrier. Not "transport is hard" but "the pass does not work before 9:30am". Not "concerts are inaccessible" but "the room drowns out the signal". Not "airlines are difficult" but "300 pounds is an arbitrary number". Not "navigation apps are limited" but "they do not know what an open drain is".

Then somebody with the power to change it agreed to change it.

That is the pattern, and it is a hopeful one. The people who meet a barrier usually already know what the fix is. Progress tends to come when they are asked, and then believed.
